## 1. Core principles

- **The author's job is to make the reader's job easy.** This is the overriding principle. Readers who have to fight to extract the point will give up, and the author loses citations or funding.
- **Success means being cited, not just being published.** Write so peers can understand and use the work.
- **Clear writing produces clear thinking**, not only the reverse. Structuring an argument for a reader tests and refines it. New insights that appear while writing are welcome; they then need to be woven into the structure (see section 9).
- **Writing is rewriting.** First drafts are supposed to be rough. Revision cycles improve both the text and the thinking.
- **Principles vs. rules.** Principles (make the reader's job easy; put the right information in the right place) must not be violated. Rules (active voice, short subjects, cut words) are guidelines; break them freely when they conflict with a principle.

## 2. Finding the story

- Science moves **data → information → knowledge → understanding**. Push the paper as far along that chain as the evidence allows. Raw data are supporting actors; the question and its larger issue are the leads.
- **Develop the story bottom-up, tell it top-down.** Explore the data widely (including outliers and non-significant hints), then cut to the simple core. Don't force data into a story decided in advance.
- Characters in science are molecules, organisms, ecosystems, processes, or concepts. "Listen" to them: let the story come from the data.

**Diagnostic:** Can the author state, in one or two sentences, what we now understand that we didn't before? If not, the problem is the story, not the prose.

## 3. SUCCES: making a story stick

| Element | In science writing | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | The core idea in a compact form (not simplistic: simplistic dodges the core) | Listing everything found, no central message |
| **Unexpected** | Expose a knowledge gap: the unknown inside the known | Showing off knowledge instead of revealing ignorance |
| **Concrete** | Specific numbers, named mechanisms, examples that ground abstractions | Middle-rung jargon with no grounding |
| **Credible** | Cited prior work, clear methods, appropriate statistics, conclusions that follow from data | Hype, boldface, buzzwords |
| **Emotional** | Curiosity (and excitement for proposals): ask a novel question | Offering information ("what's my answer?") instead of a question |
| **Stories** | Modular story units threaded into a whole | Undifferentiated mass of facts |

**Schemas:** readers learn by attaching new ideas to schemas they already hold. Build from the audience's schemas. Assuming schemas they lack confuses them; explaining ones they hold talks down to them.

**Ladder of abstraction:** top rungs (natural selection, plate tectonics) and bottom rungs (raw facts) are accessible. The middle rungs (field-specific concepts) are where jargon lives. Ground middle-rung concepts either in a widely held schema or in concrete detail.

## 4. Story structures and audience

Four elements underlie every story: **Opening** (problem, characters, setting), **Challenge** (the specific question), **Action** (what was done and found), **Resolution** (how understanding changed). A story missing any element feels unsatisfying.

| Structure | Order | Audience |
|---|---|---|
| OCAR | Opening → Challenge → Action → Resolution | Patient: specialist journals. A lead-first paper may even look like it set out to prove, not test |
| Telegraphed OCAR | Full intro, then "Here we show…" preview | Biomedical style; readers who screen quickly but still want the argument |
| ABDCE | Action (the challenge, dramatically) → Background → Development → Climax → Ending | Less patient: proposals |
| LDR | Lead (problem + challenge + key result) → Development → Resolution | Impatient but will read to the end: Nature/Science, proposals, magazines, public |
| LD | Lead → Development (no wrap-up) | Most impatient: news. Rarely right for science; proposals that end LD-style lose their final pitch |

**OCAR on IMRaD:** Introduction = opening (first paragraph) + background (funnel) + challenge (end of Introduction). Methods + Results + most of Discussion = action. The last part of the Discussion = resolution. IMRaD is a rule; OCAR is a principle. Reviews and proposals have no IMRaD, but must still cover OCAR.

**Hourglass:** wide opening → narrow challenge → narrow methods/results → widening discussion → resolution as wide as the opening. A story is a spiral: it returns to where it started, but the starting point has moved.

**Readers look for O, C, and R at their expected positions** and take whatever is there as the opening, the challenge, and the conclusion. Wrong content in those positions sends the wrong message.

## 5. The opening

The opening (first sentence to first paragraph or more) must: **identify the problem, introduce the characters, and target the audience.** Good openings can also foreshadow the challenge.

**Failure modes:**
- **Misdirection:** the first paragraph develops one mechanism, the second switches to another. Fix with a positioning sentence: "Plants control CH₄ flux by two mechanisms. The first…"
- **No direction:** textbook background that any of a hundred papers could share ("DNA carries genetic information…"). Usually the author explaining a schema to themselves. Find where the real story starts and delete everything before it.

**Targeting the audience:** the same research needs different openings for a specialist journal (methods and organisms specialists care about) vs. a generalist journal (a broad hook), or for a basic-science funder vs. a management agency.

**Two-step opening:** for a broader audience, open on an issue they care about, then narrow to yours within a paragraph. Two steps, quickly; more steps and the reader stumbles.

**Width:** the opening promises the story. Opening wider than the resolution = overpromising (readers feel cheated). Narrower = underselling (lost readers never come back). If you must err, err slightly wide and narrow quickly.

**Pawn push vs. queen launch:** a quiet positioning sentence works in patient OCAR papers if it sets up the next move. Front-loaded structures (Nature, proposals) need a strong opening claim. A pawn push with no plan behind it is throat-clearing; cut it.

## 6. The funnel: from opening to knowledge gap

The body of the Introduction connects the large problem to the specific question, narrowing step by step and framing the **knowledge gap**. Use what is known only to define the edges of what isn't.

**Failure modes:**
- **"Little is known about X."** Scientifically doubtful (there are usually many studies), logically self-defeating (followed by a list of what is known), and linguistically fuzzy (how little?). Replace with a concrete gap: "Although the broad patterns of CH₄ sources are known, their individual strengths are not."
- **The bizzwidget problem:** selling a solution (a method, model, compound) before the reader knows they have a problem. Define the problem first, show current approaches fall short, then present the solution.
- **Literature review instead of introduction:** a review builds the wall of knowledge; an introduction points at the hole in it.
  - Red flag: researchers as subjects. "Smith (2003) found X" → "X occurs (Smith 2003)".
  - Exceptions: an ongoing debate ("Although Smith (2003) reported X, Jones (2005) found Y"), or one study challenging the consensus.
  - Two camps with many papers: synthesize. "Whether X is Y (Smith 2003; Xu 2004) or Z (Arif 2005) remains unresolved."
- **Data dump:** every fact must have a concrete role in moving toward the question. By the challenge, the reader should feel the question is the obvious one to ask.

## 7. The challenge: question, hypothesis, objectives

- **The question is the core.** A hypothesis turns the question into a falsifiable prediction; objectives describe the data to be collected. Whether to frame a formal hypothesis depends on field culture (some questions, like "what is this rate constant?", have no sensible hypothesis).
- **Good challenge pattern (specialist papers): "To learn X, we did Y."** For LDR papers, a sharp, even provocative question.
- **After the question:** briefly the approach (objectives as a map). Optionally a preview of conclusions (telegraphed OCAR).

**Failure modes:**
- **Objectives only:** "Here we measured A, B, and C." → "To determine what makes T-cells anergic, we examined…"
- **Unclear question:** several implied hypotheses blurred together. Make each explicit and say how it was tested.
- **Chronological rather than intellectual order:** methods-validation goal first, the interesting question second, with strong verbs on the minor goal and weak verbs ("examined") on the main one. Lead with the main question; make validation subordinate.

## 8. Action: Methods, Results, Discussion

**Methods**
- Each method should stand on its own; "measured by the method of Lee et al. (2010)" alone forces the reader to fetch another paper.
- Lead with the purpose, then the details (LD): "To characterize enzyme inactivation, PGHS-1 was incubated with…"
- Use established names ("by the Lowry assay", "by hot-start PCR"), then note only departures from standard practice.
- Treat each method subsection as a mini-story: what information you needed, how you got it.

**Results and Discussion**
- Readers must be able to tell **what you found** from **what you think**. Three kinds of material: data, inference (robust, near-universal interpretations), and interpretation (your hypotheses about meaning).
- Results and Discussion may be combined (theoretical fields, sequential experiments, tight data–inference links), as long as the distinction stays clear. Put data before the interpretation they support; conclusion-first sentences read as imposing a plot.
- **Choose the data that tell the story.** "Murder your darlings"; archive the rest.
- **Present each result as a short LD story:** the pattern first ("Soil pH was the best predictor of bacterial diversity (r² = 0.58)"), then details and caveats.

**Statistics serve the story, not the reverse**
- Report effect size together with significance: "2.3× higher (p = 0.02)", not "significantly higher (p < 0.05)".
- A large effect with p = 0.07 is not "no effect": "2.3× higher in the treatment, though significant only at p = 0.07."
- Significance and strength differ: p = 0.001 with R² = 0.10 is a robust but weak relationship. Say both.
- The p-value is a result; declaring significance against a threshold is an interpretation.

**Discussion structure**
- The Discussion is its own story: OCAR (restate the issue, then build to the conclusion) or LDR (lead with the main finding, develop, resolve). Both work; choose deliberately.

## 9. The resolution

The ending is a power position: readers take whatever is there as the take-home message. A good resolution walks back through OCAR: **(1) synopsize the key results, (2) synthesize them to answer the question, (3) show what that contributes to the larger problem from the opening.** Ending phrases such as "the cell cycle" that echo the opening close the spiral.

**Concluding with a question** is fine if the question is concrete, grows from the results, and points forward without undermining the findings.

**Failure modes:**
- **Weak:** summarizes results, then claims importance without saying what the insight is ("may provide new insights into avian systems biology"). Fix: state the insight ("migration is physiologically stressful, and stress tolerance may limit migration distance").
- **Distracting:** textbook truisms, or a new idea never raised in the introduction. A new idea that emerged while writing should be woven back into the opening and introduction, otherwise cut.
- **Undermining:** "More research is needed", "remains to be established", "deserves further study". Reorder as "but, yes": "While further trials are needed to establish full efficacy, our data show the compound is safe and active in vivo; it may therefore offer a new treatment for…"
- **Fix procedure:** remove the dead tissue (fluff, distractions, new ideas), then do the three jobs above, concretely.

**Proposals need a resolution too.** End with a paragraph that restates the big issue, the challenge, how the pieces fit, and why it must be funded. It gives reviewers the words for their review.

## 10. Story arcs and internal structure

- A paper is a hierarchy of nested arcs: sections, subsections, paragraphs, even sentences each open, act, and resolve.
- **Complete arcs in series** are easy to follow; an idea that is discussed, dropped, and revisited across several paragraphs is hard to follow and creates repetition.
- **Arcless ("stream of consciousness") writing:** each sentence links to the previous one, but the paragraph drifts (coffee → highlands → biodiversity → deforestation → CO₂ → ocean). The problem is thematic, not local. Fix: decide the point, split into separate arcs, link them.
- Signal arcs with subheads, paragraph breaks, and flag words ("however", "consequently", "we conclude").

**Checklist:** Does each unit make one clear point? Are linkages between paragraphs clear? Has every extraneous thought been removed? Is each topic resolved before the next begins? Is every major unit marked by a subhead or a clear opening?

## 11. Paragraphs

- **Point-first** (most paragraphs; TS-D should dominate): the topic sentence makes the point, the rest develops it. A reader skimming first sentences should get the paper's essence. Extended LD: a lead of two or three sentences.
- **Point-last** (about a quarter of paragraphs, typically at openings, resolutions, and transitions): LDR (strong at both ends) or OCAR (the opening sets the question; the last sentence concludes).
- **Bad paragraphs:** several threads woven together with no visible point; an incidental character introduced as if central. Fix: (1) identify the real story, (2) choose point-first or point-last, (3) separate the threads, possibly into two paragraphs.

## 12. Sentences: topic, stress, subject–verb

- **Topic (beginning):** what the sentence is about. Should be old or familiar information. New information at the start confuses readers.
- **Stress (end):** the power position. Put the new or key information here. In multi-clause sentences, each clause end is a minor stress.
- **2–3–1 emphasis:** end (1, strongest) > beginning (2) > middle (3, weakest). Put qualifications and terms the reader probably knows in the middle.
- The same facts in different positions make different messages. Choose the order for the message you mean.
- **Subject–verb connection:** keep the verb close to its subject. Move parentheticals that separate them: "The pooled effect sizes, both with and without adjustment…, were larger" → "The pooled effect sizes were larger…, regardless of adjustment".

**Repair patterns:**
- Buried topic: "It has been predicted that global temperature will…" → "Global temperature has been predicted to…"; a long qualifying clause before the topic → move it after.
- Buried stress: words trailing after the key point ("…over 1000 products on the market") → cut the trail or move it to the middle. "…as no trend was observed" → "…as there was no clear trend" (or "as we did not observe a clear trend" to keep the caution in the middle).
- Both buried: (1) make the topic short and clear, (2) follow it at once with the main verb, (3) put the key message at the stress.

**Long sentences** are fine when well built. Straight OCAR fails in a long sentence, because the reader waits too long for the point. Use what Schimel calls a **right-opening** (right-branching) structure, an LD design: get to the topic and the main verb early, then add clauses to the right that each resolve locally. A long run-up before the main clause makes the reader hold everything without a frame.

## 13. Flow

- **Two sources of flow:** thematic coherence (all sentences on the same team) and linkage (each sentence passes a baton).
- **The Janus topic:** a good topic looks backward (familiar information) and forward (what this sentence is about).
- **Topic-to-topic = list; stress-to-topic = story.** "Molecules… Molecules… Molecules…" reads as a list. "…covalently bonded atoms. Bond strength controls… Those reactions…" reads as a story. Short lists under a clear lead are fine.
- Link by repeating a word or, more often, an idea ("bonded atoms" → "bond strength").
- **Linking paragraphs:** open a new paragraph by picking up the resolution of the previous one ("…alter particle aggregation. / Aggregation of nano-TiO₂ particles…").
- **Fix structure before flow.** If sentence 2 breaks the chain between sentences 1 and 3, sentence 2 probably belongs to another arc; move it. Forcing a local link just pushes the break down the paragraph.
- A longer subject is acceptable if it creates the link (principle over rule).

## 14. Verbs: active/passive, fuzzy verbs, nominalizations

**Active vs. passive.** Active (actor–action–acted-on) matches OCAR and is usually clearer. Use the passive when:
- the acted-on should be the topic, to keep a paragraph's character or link to the previous stress;
- the actor is unknown, unimportant, or honestly better unnamed (for example, technicians did the work).
Objectivity comes from how you treat data, not from grammatical voice. "We compared…" is acceptable and clearer than "When X and Y were compared, similar patterns were observed."

**Fuzzy verbs** say something happened but not what: affect, facilitate, occur, perform, conduct, implement, and often mediate, evaluate, influence, play a role in. Replace them with action verbs that show the mechanism: "Herbivores facilitate invasion by mediating competition" → "Herbivores preferentially eat native plants, giving exotic grasses a competitive advantage."

**Fuzzy hypotheses** are the worst case: "Community composition is controlled by plant inputs, water, and soil chemistry" is a truism. Make it testable: "The chemistry of plant inputs is the single strongest control on community composition." A scientist's job is not to be right but to be clear and testable.

**Nominalizations** turn actions into nouns and force weak verbs: "conducted an investigation of" → "investigated"; "a failure to reproduce the data is often observed" → "they often fail to reproduce the data"; "There was a difference between" → "differed". Adjective nominalizations ("the characteristics… are the oxidation of…") → "characterized by oxidized…".

Nominalizations are useful when they **name a concept the reader holds** (gene expression, ecosystem services) or **sum up earlier text** ("these arguments", "these limitations"). For readers without the schema, they become jargon.

## 15. Words: jargon, register, compound nouns

- **Jargon** = a term for a schema the reader doesn't hold, or one with an adequate plain equivalent. A **technical term** = a term the reader holds, or one with no plain equivalent. The same word can be either, depending on the audience.
- **Placement of terms:** at the beginning, you assume everyone knows it (risk: it reads as jargon). At the end, you define it for everyone (risk: it seems you think it's new). In the middle, often as a short parenthetical ("Programmed cell death, or apoptosis, is…"), you remind without lecturing. If unsure, over-define: an expert's mild irritation costs less than a novice giving up.
- **Acronyms:** spell out at first use unless universal (DNA). Name things for the reader's convenience ("deciduous/conifer"), not the author's ("Site A/Site B").
- **Unnecessarily technical:** "terrestrial autotrophs" when "plants" is meant; "climate regime" for "climate"; "a causative agent of" for "causes".
- **Register:** prefer short common words to long Latinate ones when the meaning is the same: utilize → use, initiate → start, demonstrate → show, permit → let, consume → eat (unless the nuance matters), methodology → method, duration → length, prior to → before. Test: would you say it to a colleague over coffee?
- **Compound nouns vs. prepositional phrases:** two nouns: prefer the compound ("reaction rate", not "rate of the reaction"). Four or more: break up the noun train. Three: judge by ear and clarity ("resource allocation modification" is bad). Watch for noun trains that make a noun look like a verb ("community composition influences are…"). A prepositional phrase is right when it puts a key word in the stress position ("limit the effects of global warming").

## 16. Condensing

**Strategy: prune the big limbs, then shake out the dead leaves.**
1. Decide what doesn't need to be said, and cut whole blocks (selection, not compression).
2. Repair broken arcs; scattered ideas repeat themselves.
3. Then cut word-level filler. Typical first drafts lose 10–20% (sometimes 50%) without losing information.

Formatting tricks (smaller font, crammed figures) are not condensing.

**Targets:**
- **Redundancy:** "develop, test, and apply"; "most, but not all"; "proof-of-principle for the feasibility".
- **Obvious:** "There is evidence that X (ref)" (the citation is the evidence; "can" already hedges); "high alpine"; "a characteristic of".
- **Empty amplifiers:** very, highly, dramatically, entirely, certainly, substantially, quite, really. Replace with the number: "increased X by a factor of 42". Watch for modifiers that hide missing substance ("a highly effective control mechanism that efficiently discriminates…": what mechanism?) and catch-alls ("structural and functional changes": which?).
- **Good modifiers distinguish or change meaning:** "the first phase", "a preliminary result" (vs. the empty "a final result").
- **Metadiscourse:** "We found that…", "In this study, we measured…", "It is worth noting that…". Keep it where it signals structure ("We conclude that…") or distinguishes past work ("In a previous study, we…").
- **Verbosity:** "some enhancement in the applicability of these measurements can be accomplished with…" → "Freeze-fracture pre-treatment improved…".
- **Verbs:** converting nominalizations to verbs shortens almost every sentence.

**Don't kill every word.** Words that build flow and coherence are working. "Writing can get too barren. Filler can add flow. Use it carefully." is shorter and worse.

## 17. Limitations: "but, yes"

- "Yes, but" (results, then limitations) makes the limitation the last word, the take-home message. Use **"but, yes"**: state the limitation, then the finding, and end on the finding.
- **Introduction:** many "limitations" are a mismatch between question and method. Revise the question to the one the method can answer (agar plating cannot count all soil bacteria, but can count those able to respond to fresh substrate), and adjust the opening to match.
- **Methods:** address methodological constraints right where the method is described, plainly and without apology (why an exclosure could not be replicated, and how variation was handled). In proposals, add a "potential pitfalls" section; reviewers forgive limitations the authors have clearly thought about.
- "There is no bad method, only bad interpretation": define what the method actually measures.
- **Discussion:** discuss limitations briefly, early in the body, not in the opening or concluding power positions. Exception: for novel or artificial setups, open the Discussion with an "experimental considerations" section that calibrates the reader before the results.
- **In the conclusion**, if limitations must appear: "Despite these limitations, the methods represent a major advance…" A summarizing nominalization ("these limitations") acknowledges them and moves on.

## 18. Other audiences

**International journals (authors writing in English as a second language)**
- Story and structure matter more than grammar: editors send back a good story in rough English for language fixes, but reject a weak story in polished English. OCAR is not about English.
- Novelty means new knowledge, not new information. An old question in a new system, with new technology, or both, only fills in the information base. Applied work also needs broader insight.
- Choose a journal whose audience fits the story. Authors (including English-speaking coauthors) are responsible for the language; editors and reviewers will not fix it.

**The public**
- A simple story, focused on "who knew?" or "what it means for you/society"; people (the researchers) are engaging characters.
- Front-loaded (LDR) structure.
- Define or avoid every technical term; never introduce one in the topic position. Readers are intelligent but uninformed: don't patronize.
- Present science as a process of many incremental steps, not "Eureka" breakthroughs.
- **Message box:** Issue and Audience (overarching), then (1) Problem, (2) So what?, (3) Solution, (4) Benefits. In LDR, (1) and (2) go into the lead. Example: a gene-cascade researcher answering "are you going to cure cancer?" starts from that schema and then explains honestly which piece of the puzzle the work provides.
- The message box works for peers too, especially proposal openings.

**Policy makers**
- Understanding the science is not enough to move decisions; their culture, goals, and constraints differ. Be extremely brief (a five-minute hallway conversation), frame the message in their goals, and know what decision is being made.
