# Section-by-section revision guide

Checklists for diagnosing each part of a paper or proposal. Each item points to the relevant section of `principles.md` (§N). Work through the questions before rewriting; report the failures you find, most structural first.

## Contents

- Title and abstract
- Introduction
- Methods
- Results
- Discussion and conclusion
- Grant proposals (general; NSF/NIH-style)
- NSFC (国家自然科学基金) proposals
- Cover letters and plain-language summaries

---

## Title and abstract

The abstract is a whole paper in miniature: one or two sentences each for O, C, A, R. This section applies the book's principles; the book itself doesn't treat abstracts separately.

- Does the first sentence state the problem this paper addresses, not generic field background? (§5)
- Is the question explicit, or only "we investigated…"? (§7)
- Are results concrete, with effect sizes and key numbers, not "significantly affected"? (§8)
- Does the last sentence give the conclusion and its contribution to the opening problem, not "these results provide insights into…" or "further research is needed"? (§9)
- Is the width consistent? Don't open on global climate if the conclusion is about primer efficiency. (§5)
- Title: does it carry the main finding or question, in words a broad reader can parse?

## Introduction

- **Opening (first sentence to first paragraph):** problem, characters, audience. No textbook background, no misdirection. Is it pitched at the right width for the journal? (§5)
- **Funnel:** does each step narrow toward the question? Is every fact there to outline the gap, not to show coverage? (§6)
- **Gap:** stated concretely, not "little is known" / "remains unclear" / "研究较少"? (§6)
- **Citations:** findings as subjects with citations in parentheses, except for debates? (§6)
- **Solution before problem?** Is a method, model, or material introduced before the reader knows why it is needed? (§6)
- **Challenge (end of Introduction):** an explicit question or hypothesis, then the approach: "To learn X, we did Y". Main goal first. (§7)
- **Hypotheses:** falsifiable, not truisms? (§14)
- **For Nature/Science-style papers:** is the lead strong enough to survive editorial triage? (§4, §5)

## Methods

- Does each subsection open with its purpose before the details? (§8)
- Does it stand on its own: a short description plus the citation, not a bare citation? (§8)
- Named standard methods, with only the departures spelled out? (§8)
- Methodological limitations addressed right where the method appears, plainly? (§17)
- Is the passive used for good reason (the material is the topic, the actor is irrelevant), or reflexively? "We" is acceptable when the authors did the work. (§14)

## Results

- Does each paragraph or subsection lead with the pattern (LD), then the details? (§8)
- Are effect sizes reported alongside p-values? Is "not significant" used for large effects with marginal p? Are significance and strength (R²) distinguished? (§8)
- Is the data shown only the data needed for the story? (§8)
- In combined Results/Discussion: do data come before the inferences drawn from them, with signal words ("indicating", "demonstrates")? (§8)
- Sentences: are the key numbers in the stress position? (§12)

## Discussion and conclusion

- Is the Discussion a story of its own, OCAR or LDR, chosen deliberately? (§8)
- Does the opening of the Discussion restate the question or lead with the main finding, rather than repeat results or start with limitations? (§8, §17)
- Are limitations placed early in the body, brief and honest, with "but, yes" ordering? (Exception: an "experimental considerations" opening for novel setups.) (§17)
- Does each paragraph make one point (complete arcs), or does a theme recur in scattered paragraphs? (§10, §11)
- **Final paragraph:** synopsize → synthesize (answer the question) → contribution to the opening problem. Same width as the opening. (§9)
- Is any idea raised for the first time at the end? (§9)
- Does it end on "more research is needed" or a self-undermining caveat? (§9)
- A concluding question is fine if it is concrete and grows from the results. (§9)

## Grant proposals (general; NSF/NIH-style)

Reviewers are the least patient audience. They often make a no/maybe call by the end of the introduction, and a good design never rescues boring questions.

- **First page / Specific Aims:** front-loaded (ABDCE or LDR). Within the first paragraph: the important problem, the gap, and the exciting question. (§4)
- **Panel audience:** reviewers come from neighboring subfields; use a two-step opening that starts from a concern most panelists share. (§5)
- **Questions, not tasks:** each aim tied to a question or falsifiable hypothesis, not "we will measure…". (§7, §14)
- **Background is not a literature review:** every paragraph moves toward the specific aims. (§6)
- **Potential pitfalls / alternative approaches:** limitations addressed where the method is described, showing they have been thought through. (§17)
- **Resolution paragraph:** restate the big issue, how the aims fit together to address it, and the expected impact. Proposals that stop after the last experiment lose their final pitch. (§9)
- **Page limits:** condense by pruning blocks and fixing arcs before shrinking fonts. (§16)

## NSFC (国家自然科学基金) proposals

Map the NSFC sections onto the story elements. This is an application of the book's framework, not part of the book.

| NSFC section | Story role | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| 立项依据（研究意义、国内外研究现状及发展动态分析） | Opening + funnel | Does it open with a problem panelists care about, not a policy slogan or textbook background? Does the 研究现状 frame the gap (the hole in the wall), rather than list who did what? Are "研究较少" / "尚不清楚" replaced by a concrete unknown? (§5, §6) |
| 科学问题 / 拟解决的关键科学问题 | Challenge | Phrased as a genuine scientific question (what mechanism, what controls, under what conditions), not an engineering task or a list of measurements? (§7) |
| 研究目标 | Challenge → approach | Goals expressed as knowledge to gain ("阐明……的机制"), main goal first, with validation goals subordinate? (§7) |
| 研究内容 | Action (plan) | Does each content item map to the question, forming complete arcs? (§10) |
| 研究方案及可行性分析 | Action + limitations | Technical risks addressed where they arise ("but, yes"), with alternatives? (§17) |
| 特色与创新之处 | Unexpected (SUCCES) | Novelty in the question and insight, not only in a new system or a new instrument? (§3, §18) |
| 预期研究成果 / 结尾 | Resolution | Returns to the problem raised in 立项依据, at the same width; states what understanding will change. (§9) |

Common Chinese-language issues to fix:
- "对……进行了系统研究/深入分析" → a concrete verb with its object (§14).
- Long chains of "的" (the Chinese noun train): "土壤微生物群落结构组成变化的驱动机制的研究" → break it up or restructure with a verb (§15).
- Stacked empty amplifiers: "具有十分重要的科学意义和广阔的应用前景" → say what the significance is (§16).
- Put the new and key information at the end of the sentence; begin with the known topic (§12).

## Cover letters and plain-language summaries

- **Cover letter to a generalist journal:** an LD/LDR lead stating the question, the key finding, and why it matters beyond the subfield, in the first two or three sentences. (§4)
- **Plain-language summary / public outreach:** build a message box (Problem → So what? → Solution → Benefits), LDR structure, no undefined terms, no terms in topic position, human characters where possible. (§18)
